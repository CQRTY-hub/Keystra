## What this is

Keystra sells Steam keys, gift cards and in-game top-up codes to European consumers.
One person, based in Belgium. Not a marketplace — I buy from a distributor and sell
directly, so every product on the site is mine.

## Who is buying, and in what state of mind

Someone on a phone, often late, deciding whether this site is a scam before they decide
whether they want the game. They already know what they want — they came from a search or
a social post. The job is not to persuade them to buy; it's to get out of their way and
give them no reason to close the tab.

They have been burned before, or know someone who has. Every design decision is read
through that filter.

## Mode per page

- **Home, shop, product pages** — persuade, but quietly. Show the product, the price, the
  region, and get to the buy button.
- **Checkout, order confirmation, order lookup** — operate. No decoration, no upsell,
  nothing that isn't the task.

## Brand voice

Three words: **calm, direct, legitimate.**

I never sound excited. No exclamation marks, no urgency, no "grab it now", no countdowns.
The tone of a shop that expects to still be here in five years and doesn't need this
particular sale.

## Anti-references — what I refuse to look like

Concrete, from real competitors:

- **A shop that talks about itself before it shows the product.** Kinguin opens with a
  festival banner, then a second full-screen festival block, then a mascot asking how it
  can help — before a single game appears.
- **Permanent banners and mascot chat bubbles** that follow you across pages and steal
  screen space you never get back. G2A's promo bar, Kinguin's cartoon penguin.
- **Discount badges on everything.** −95%, −92%, −42% side by side. When everything is
  discounted, discount means nothing.
- **Struck-through prices nobody believes** — €69.99 down to €1.25. Reads as a fabricated
  original price, and EU pricing rules agree.
- **Ads above the buy button.** G2A puts "SPONSORED" in the most valuable space on the
  product page.
- **Inconsistent product imagery** — different aspect ratios, different sharpness,
  compressed until grainy. AllKeyShop's grid.
- **Filter menus you navigate three clicks deep** before seeing a product.
- **Cookie banners that assume consent** by continued browsing.
- Purple gradients, neon glow, cyan-on-black, mascots, stock photos of gamers in headsets.

## Visual direction

**Light base, navy structure, gold only for buying.**

- `#FFFFFF` and `#E5E5E5` — page background and surfaces. The shop is light and open.
- `#14213D` — deep navy. Header, footer, and one dark accent block per page where the
  weight belongs. This is the structural colour.
- `#FCA311` — gold. **Buy actions only.** Not on badges, not on labels, not on links. The
  moment gold appears somewhere that doesn't take money, the whole system reads as a
  discount site.
- `#000000` — text.

No second accent colour. No gradients. No glow.

**Why:** navy is the colour of banks and insurers, not of clearance sites. Gold touches
"key" and "worth keeping" without turning it into a game. Light rather than dark is
deliberate — every competitor in this category is dark, so light is immediately distinct
and reads as open rather than hidden.

## The hard constraint: I don't control my own product imagery

Boxart comes from publishers via my distributor. It is loud, inconsistent, and clashes with
itself. The design has to survive that.

- **One fixed aspect ratio and one fixed card shape** for every product. The card frames
  the artwork instead of competing with it.
- A light, uniform card surface so that chaotic art still reads as an orderly grid.
- A minimum image quality standard — a product without usable artwork doesn't get published.
- **Illustration for everything that IS mine**: empty states, redemption guides, error
  pages, the maintenance page. That's where the brand actually gets to speak.

Reference for how this feels done right: Nexora — light background, one dark block carrying
the weight, uniform product cards, a single accent colour used sparingly. And Firewatch for
the discipline of one colour world with no exceptions.

## Trust posture

I sell a product people expect to be scammed on. These are design problems, not legal ones:

- **Region locks and platform restrictions visible before purchase**, on the product page —
  never discovered in the terms.
- **Delivery timing stated plainly.**
- **The refund and faulty-key procedure explained in advance**, including what evidence is
  needed and how long it takes. Setting the expectation honestly beats arguing later.
- **A real named person behind the shop**, based in Belgium. Not a stock-photo team.
- The key on the confirmation page needs a copy button. On a phone, selecting a long key
  string with a thumb is close to impossible — and this is the most important moment in the
  entire transaction.

## Mobile is the primary device

Traffic comes from TikTok and Instagram. Design at phone width first, then widen. Tap
targets at least 44px, especially the consent checkboxes. Nothing scrolls horizontally.
